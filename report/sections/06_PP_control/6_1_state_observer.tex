\paragraph{Why an observer}
Only the ball position and the coil current are directly measured on the real setup, while the ball
velocity is required by the state-feedback controller. A numerical derivative of the measured position
would amplify sensor noise and would make the feedback action less robust. For this reason, a
Luenberger observer is introduced on the linearized mechanical subsystem. The measured current is used
as a known input to the mechanical model, while the measured position is used to correct the estimated
state.

In \texttt{SS\_model\_PP.m} the observer poles are placed at
\begin{equation}
    p_{obs} = \{-500, -600\}.
\end{equation}
Those poles are chosen to be significantly faster than the closed-loop poles of the system,
in order to ensure a good estimation performance.

The observer gain is computed with
\begin{equation}
    L_{obs} = \mathrm{place}(A_x^T, C_x^T, p_{obs})^T.
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Ackermann's formula}
Since $(A_x,C_x)$ is observable, the dual pair $(A_x^T,C_x^T)$ is controllable, also the subsystem is SI(Single Input),
therefore Ackermann's formula can be applied to compute the observer gain. Defining
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_d =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        C_x^T & A_x^T C_x^T
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \phi_{obs}(s) =
    (s-p_{obs,1})(s-p_{obs,2}),
\end{equation}
the observer gain is obtained as
\begin{equation}
    L_{obs}^T =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \mathcal{C}_d^{-1}
    \phi_{obs}(A_x^T).
\end{equation}

The resulting observer dynamics are
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\hat{\vect{x}}}
    =
    (A_x - L_{obs} C_x)\hat{\vect{x}}
    +
    B_x \Delta i_{\mathrm{meas}}
    +
    L_{obs} \Delta x_{\mathrm{meas}}.
\end{equation}
